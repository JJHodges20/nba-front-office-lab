# How Far Can a Team Go?

The postseason model: how likely each stage of the playoffs is, for a team in the bracket and for a team of a given strength before the bracket is known - and how such estimates have come out on seasons the model had not seen. Generated by `fol playoffs report --write` from revision 1 of the model (fitted 2026-10-04) and build 13; do not edit it by hand - fit the model again, rebuild, and run the command again.

> Every figure here is an estimate with an interval, scored against what happened on seasons the model was not fitted to. A chance of 60% means that, of the teams given about 60%, about 60% got there - the reliability tables say how close that has held. Nothing here is a guarantee, and nothing is a verdict on a team.

## What the model is

Two models share one file; both read two things about a team, each known before its first playoff game:

- **Net rating**: the team's regular-season points scored less allowed per 100 possessions, as the league publishes it.
- **Top-player quality**: the mean impact, by the value model, of the 3 players with the highest impact who finished the regular season with the team - the players a playoff rotation leans on.

**The series model** is a model of one playoff game - the log-odds that the home side wins are a home advantage plus a weight per point of the gap in net rating plus a weight per point of the gap in top-player quality - carried through the series format (best of seven in the 2-2-1-1-1 pattern, with the formats of earlier seasons as they were) by enumerating how a series can go. Set the sixteen playoff teams in their bracket and it gives every team's chance of every round, exactly, because the two sides of any later matchup are decided independently.

**The season model** is for a team before the bracket is known - a roster being built, a season not yet played. It is the roadmap's hierarchy: the chance of making the playoffs for any team, of winning a round for a playoff team, of the conference finals for a team that won a round, and so on to the title, each a logistic in the same two inputs; the chance of a stage is the steps up to it multiplied together.

Nothing else is in either model. What else was tried, and what it did on seasons the model had not seen, is in the tables below; the reasons for what was kept and left out are in `docs/decisions/0014-postseason-model.md`.

## How it was tested

The model is fitted on every season on record (1996-97 to 2025-26: 2,440 playoff games, 450 series, 892 team-seasons). To test it, each season from 2006-07 on was scored with parameters fitted to the seasons before it alone - the series model on its series, then carried through the season's actual bracket and scored on where every playoff team got to, and the season model on every team-season. That is 300 series, 320 team-postseasons and 600 team-seasons the model had not seen when it scored them.

Two scores are used. **Log loss** is the average surprise at what happened: a model sure of a thing that did not happen pays dearly, and a coin flip at every series scores 0.693. **Brier** is the average squared gap between the chance given and what happened (0 or 1). Lower is better for both. A **calibration slope** near 1 says the chances meant what they said across the range; below 1, the model was too sure of itself at the extremes. Intervals come from refitting on 2,000 resamples of whole seasons.

## What this does not say

- The inputs are the regular season's. A star hurt in April, a trade at the deadline or a rotation that changes for the playoffs is seen only as far as it shows in net rating and in who finished the season with the team.
- The season model knows nothing of the field: a team's conference, and how strong the other teams are that season, are not in it. The gap to the conference's eighth-best rating was tried and helps; it is left out because the model exists to be read for a roster before a season, when the field is not known.
- The play-in is not modelled. A team that reaches the bracket through it has made the playoffs like any other.
- Home court in a hypothetical matchup follows the league's main tie-breaks (record, head to head, seed); the league's full order goes further.
- The series model's parameters have moved across eras - home advantage has fallen - and the model is fitted to every season at once. The era table shows how far, and the calibration by era how much it costs.
- A probability is an estimate with an interval. The reliability tables are the evidence for reading it as one; they are not a promise about any one team.
